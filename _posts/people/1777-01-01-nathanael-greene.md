**Alternative names**: Nathanael Greene, General Greene, "The Fighting Quaker"

**Birth**: August 7, 1742, Potowomut (Warwick), Rhode Island  
**Death**: June 19, 1786, Mulberry Grove Plantation, Georgia  
**Rank**: Major General, Continental Army  
**Notable roles**: Commander, Army of Observation, Rhode Island (1775); Major General, Continental Army (1776–1783); Quartermaster General of the Continental Army (February 25, 1778 – August 1780); Commander, Southern Department (December 3, 1780 – 1783)

## Summary

General Nathanael Greene (1742–1786) rose from a Rhode Island ironmonger's family to become George Washington's ablest subordinate and the architect of the strategy that broke British will in the South. Unlike the aristocratic officers who populated European armies of the period, Greene was self-taught, self-made, and devoutly practical — three qualities that made him ideally suited to the improvised, resource-starved Continental Army.

In 1777 specifically, Greene served as a Major General engaged in the campaigns around Philadelphia — the disastrous defeats at Brandywine Creek (September 11, 1777) and Germantown (October 4, 1777) — and contributed strategic advice during the Valley Forge encampment (December 1777 – June 1778). His appointment as Quartermaster General in February 1778 gave him the logistical command that would rescue the Continental Army from dissolution, while his simultaneous strategic thinking laid the groundwork for the unconventional southern strategy that would eventually end the war.

## Early Life and Quaker Upbringing

Nathanael Greene was born August 7, 1742, at the family forge and farm in Potowomut (now part of Warwick), Rhode Island. His father, Nathanael Greene Sr., was a prosperous Quaker ironmaster who expected his son to manage the forge. The elder Greene's Quaker convictions shaped the household: formal military training was forbidden, and even the study of military theory was discouraged as incompatible with Quaker pacifism.

Despite this, young Greene educated himself voraciously. He taught himself Latin and Greek, read widely in history and philosophy, and — in the years before the Revolution — began secretly acquiring books on military theory. An early influence was John Adams, whom Greene met through Rhode Island's political network; Adams encouraged his intellectual curiosity and connected him with Boston's circle of constitutional theorists.

By the early 1770s, Greene had become convinced that colonial resistance to British taxation was just — a conviction that placed him in direct conflict with his Quaker meeting's pacifism. When he joined the Kentish Guards (a local militia company) in 1774, the Quakers expelled him. The expulsion cost him his religious community but committed him fully to the revolutionary cause.

## Early Revolutionary Service (1775–1776)

When the Continental Congress established the Continental Army in June 1775, Greene was commissioned as brigadier general commanding the Rhode Island Army of Observation — making him, at 33, one of the youngest general officers in the Continental Army. Washington, observing Greene at the Siege of Boston, quickly identified him as unusually capable: intelligent, methodical, and free of the vanity and status-seeking that plagued some senior officers.

By 1776, Washington trusted Greene enough to assign him the defense of Fort Washington on Manhattan (November 1776) — a decision that ended in disaster when the British captured the fort and 2,800 Continental soldiers. The defeat haunted Greene, who had urged Washington to hold the fort rather than evacuate it. Rather than destroying his reputation, the defeat seemed to intensify his determination; Washington's decision to retain him despite the disaster demonstrated the depth of his trust.

## The 1777 Campaign: Brandywine and Germantown

Greene's most significant contributions in 1777 came during the Philadelphia campaign.

### Battle of Brandywine (September 11, 1777)

At Brandywine Creek on September 11, 1777, British General Howe flanked Washington's position by crossing an unguarded ford (Jeffrie's Ford) several miles upstream, threatening to envelop the American right. Washington sent Greene's division on a rapid cross-country march — approximately four miles in forty-five minutes — to plug the gap and cover the retreat of Sullivan's crumbling right flank. Greene's troops arrived in time to form a rear-guard position that allowed thousands of men to escape what could have been a catastrophic encirclement. His steady performance under pressure preserved the Continental Army as a fighting force and demonstrated his capacity to maneuver rapidly under fire.

### Battle of Germantown (October 4, 1777)

Washington's ambitious assault on Germantown — a night march followed by a coordinated four-column attack — failed partly due to fog and coordination problems. Greene commanded the left column (the largest), which arrived late due to a longer march route. Despite the defeat, Greene's column penetrated furthest into the British position, demonstrating the army's growing tactical capability even in failure. Post-battle analysis by Washington and his officers — including the pivotal question of whether attacking with multiple converging columns was feasible for an army with the Continental Army's coordination problems — influenced planning for future operations.

## Quartermaster General: The Logistical Rescue (1778)

Washington appointed Greene Quartermaster General on February 25, 1778, an appointment Greene accepted reluctantly. He had sought a field command; the quartermaster role seemed administrative and unglamorous. Washington's argument to him was unambiguous: the army was dissolving for want of supplies, and only a general officer with Greene's energy and reputation could reverse the crisis.

The Valley Forge encampment (December 19, 1777 – June 19, 1778) had reduced the Continental Army to near-collapse. By February 1778, approximately 2,000 soldiers had died from disease, exposure, and malnutrition; another 3,500 were classified as unfit for duty. Horses died at a rate that threatened the army's ability to move artillery. The situation stemmed not from absolute scarcity but from systemic failure: quartermaster records were chaotic, contractors were corrupt, payment in depreciating Continental currency drove farmers to sell to British forces in Philadelphia for hard sterling, and transportation networks had broken down.

Greene's reforms were methodical:

1. **Decentralized procurement.** He established regional deputy quartermasters — men with local knowledge and farmer relationships — rather than relying on a central bureau in Philadelphia. Each region was responsible for specific supply categories.

2. **Transportation infrastructure.** He organized wagon teams and built supply road networks, ensuring supplies moved from rural procurement points to Valley Forge reliably. Within weeks, supply convoys were arriving regularly.

3. **Financial management.** Greene negotiated contracts that offered partial payment in goods rather than paper currency, making deals feasible for farmers skeptical of Continental scrip. He also conducted a systematic audit of quartermaster accounts — unprecedented in the army's brief history.

By June 1778, the army that marched from Valley Forge to pursue Clinton's column toward New York was a different institution than the one that had wintered there. Greene's logistics work was as essential to this transformation as von Steuben's military drilling, though it received less historical attention.

## Strategic Thinking: The Philosophy of Unconventional Warfare

As Quartermaster General, Greene had unusual access to the full strategic picture: supply routes, terrain, state political dynamics, militia capacities, and British vulnerabilities. From this vantage point, he developed a strategic philosophy that would define the southern campaign:

**Principle 1: Preserve the army above all else.** A destroyed army could not be rebuilt; a retreating army remained a threat. Conventional European doctrine demanded pitched battles; American resources demanded avoiding them until advantages were overwhelming.

**Principle 2: Use terrain and supply lines as weapons.** The American interior offered vast territory across which the British could not maintain supply lines without disproportionate force. Long British supply lines were vulnerable to militia interdiction. The strategic goal was to force the British to extend further than their resources allowed.

**Principle 3: Integrate militia and regulars.** European armies dismissed irregular militia as militarily useless. Greene understood that militia — though unreliable in pitched battle — was decisive in controlling countryside, disrupting British logistics, and gathering intelligence. The combination of a small Continental regular force (to anchor the line and fix British regulars) and active militia (to harass supply and isolate detachments) was more powerful than either alone.

These ideas challenged the assumptions of French advisors like Rochambeau and contradicted the European-trained expectations of officers like Charles Lee. Washington grasped their validity and gave Greene extraordinary latitude.

## The Southern Campaign (1780–1781)

When Washington appointed Greene commander of the Southern Department on December 3, 1780, the situation was desperate. The British had captured Charleston, South Carolina (May 12, 1780) with the surrender of 5,500 Continental troops — the worst American defeat of the war. General Horatio Gates had then suffered catastrophic defeat at Camden, South Carolina (August 16, 1780), losing another 2,000 men. The Continental Army in the South barely existed.

Greene arrived in Charlotte, North Carolina, to find 2,307 soldiers, of whom only 1,482 were fit for duty. Artillery was minimal; food and clothing were critically short.

His response was unconventional. Rather than consolidating his small force, he split it — sending Brigadier General Daniel Morgan west while he moved east, forcing the British under Lord Cornwallis to also split their force to counter both threats.

### Battle of Cowpens (January 17, 1781)

Morgan's command engaged British Lieutenant Colonel Banastre Tarleton at Cowpens, South Carolina, on January 17, 1781, employing the militia-and-regulars integration Greene had theorized. Morgan placed militia in the front line with orders to fire twice and withdraw — predictable behavior that lured Tarleton forward — then hit the pursuing British with Continental regulars in a planned double envelopment. The result was one of the most complete tactical victories of the war: Tarleton lost 110 killed, 200 wounded, and 500 captured of approximately 1,100 engaged; Morgan's casualties were 12 killed and 60 wounded.

### Battle of Guilford Courthouse (March 15, 1781)

At Guilford Courthouse, North Carolina, on March 15, 1781, Greene deliberately accepted a tactical defeat to achieve strategic victory. He positioned his forces in a similar three-line arrangement to Cowpens, forcing Cornwallis to attack uphill through three defensive positions. The British drove Greene from the field, but at ruinous cost: of approximately 1,900 British engaged, 93 were killed and 413 wounded — a 27% casualty rate in a single engagement. Greene's losses were higher in killed and wounded, but his army remained intact and able to continue the campaign.

The British member of Parliament Charles James Fox remarked, upon reading Cornwallis's dispatch: "Another such victory would ruin the British Army." Cornwallis, unable to sustain such losses while maintaining his position in North Carolina, turned north toward Virginia — directly into the trap that would close at Yorktown.

## Post-War Life and Legacy

Greene returned to Georgia after the war, where Congress granted him Mulberry Grove Plantation — confiscated from a Loyalist — as partial compensation for personal debts he had incurred supplying his army. Congress never fully reimbursed him for the approximately $400,000 in personal credit he had pledged to obtain army supplies during the southern campaign.

He died on June 19, 1786, at Mulberry Grove from sunstroke, aged 43, just three years after the war ended. His death was sudden and widely mourned; Washington wrote that Greene's loss was "irreparable."

Greene's reputation grew dramatically in the following century. Military historians ranked him among the most capable generals of the eighteenth century — not for spectacular victories but for strategic coherence: the ability to maintain an army, manage logistics, and pursue a campaign with consistent strategic purpose across years of adversity. His approach to the Southern Campaign — the "southern strategy of exhaustion" — became a model for subsequent American irregular warfare doctrine.

Greeneville, Tennessee, and Greenville, South Carolina, are among numerous places named in his honor. Fort Greene in Brooklyn, New York, preserves his name in the city where he served early in the war.
