## Summary

On April 25–27, 1777, British General William Tryon, the Loyalist royal governor of New York, led a raiding expedition from New York into Connecticut, targeting the American supply depot at Danbury. The operation burned the depot's substantial stores of provisions, uniforms, and military equipment, disrupting American logistics for months. The raid provoked an immediate militia response: Connecticut and New York forces converged under the command of Generals David Wooster and [Benedict Arnold]({{ '/news/people/benedict-arnold/' | relative_url }}), intercepting Tryon's retreat and engaging him in a running battle through Ridgefield, Connecticut on April 27. Though Tryon's force ultimately escaped to their ships, the Danbury Raid exemplified the Revolutionary War's devastating coastal periphery operations and demonstrated the vulnerability of American supply lines to British naval and amphibious reach.

## The Raid: April 25–26, 1777

Tryon planned the Danbury Raid as a strike into the American heartland to destroy supplies and demonstrate British power in the Connecticut interior. The target was Danbury, in western Connecticut, where the Continental Army had accumulated food, flour, uniforms, and military equipment in civilian storage — an attractive but exposed magazine protected only by local militia and Continental detachments. Tryon assembled a force of approximately 1,500 soldiers drawn from the Royal Artillery, several British regiments, and Loyalist companies. On April 25, the force was transported from New York across Long Island Sound by the British Navy, landed near Compo Beach in Westport, Connecticut, and marched inland to Danbury.

The American defenders, caught unprepared by the speed and scale of the incursion, evacuated the depot as the British approached. By the evening of April 25, Tryon's soldiers had occupied Danbury and set the supplies on fire. Warehouses burning through the night released smoke and flames visible for miles, signaling the American forces that a major raid was underway. The supply losses were staggering: contemporary American accounts itemized approximately 1,600 tents, 4,000 barrels of beef and pork, 100 hogsheads of rum, 2,000 bushels of grain, and large stocks of military hardware, clothing, and medicine — stores the Continental Army had been accumulating since the previous autumn. The total loss represented a substantial share of the army's strategic reserve in the northeastern theater and significantly hampered planned spring operations.

## The American Response: April 26–27

As news of the raid spread through Connecticut and New York, militia forces mobilized rapidly. General David Wooster, commander of Connecticut's militia, called out forces from Ridgefield, Danbury, Norwalk, and surrounding towns. [General Benedict Arnold]({{ '/news/people/benedict-arnold/' | relative_url }}), stationed nearby with Continental troops, moved to intercept Tryon's withdrawal. The two generals planned a coordinated strategy: delay Tryon's retreat and harass his column while gathering sufficient forces for a decisive engagement.

On April 27, as Tryon's force began its march back toward the coast, American militia and Continental regulars attacked the British column near Ridgefield, Connecticut. The ensuing engagement — known as the Battle of Ridgefield — was not a set-piece battle but a series of running skirmishes and ambushes as Tryon's disciplined soldiers fought their way through American resistance. The battle was fierce and confused, with militiamen firing from stone walls, orchards, and roadside positions while British soldiers formed ranks and drove forward with bayonets fixed.

The American forces under Arnold and Wooster fought tenaciously but could not break Tryon's column. British discipline and superior organization prevailed against militia bravery. However, the American harassment inflicted a steady toll in casualties — American Battlefield Trust records indicate approximately 26 British killed and 117 wounded, with around 20 American killed and 40–80 wounded — and significantly impeded Tryon's retreat.

General David Wooster was mortally wounded during the fighting at Bethel, Connecticut on April 27, 1777 — struck by a musket ball while rallying his militia forces to pursue the British column. He died nine days later, on May 2, 1777, at Danbury, becoming one of the most senior American officers killed in action during the war. His death was a severe blow to Connecticut's command structure but transformed him into a martyr figure that strengthened recruitment and patriotic resolve across the region.

General Benedict Arnold, commanding on horseback at Ridgefield, had his horse shot and killed beneath him during a close-range British volley. Arnold disentangled himself from the fallen animal and continued fighting on foot — an act of conspicuous bravery that was widely reported and contributed to Congress reinstating his seniority among Major Generals, a rank dispute that had frustrated him for months. Colonel John Lamb, commanding the Continental artillery alongside Arnold, was wounded in the face and lost the sight of one eye during the engagement.

By April 27 afternoon, Tryon's force reached the coast and reboarded their ships at Compo Beach, escaping back across Long Island Sound to New York. The raid had achieved its immediate objective — destroying American supplies — but the vigorous militia response and running battle at Ridgefield prevented the operation from becoming a bloodless British triumph and signaled that the interior of Connecticut was not as defenseless as British strategists had hoped.

## Strategic Significance for 1777

The Danbury Raid was one of several British operations in spring 1777 designed to strike deep into American territory and disrupt supply lines while [General Burgoyne]({{ '/news/people/general-john-burgoyne/' | relative_url }}) prepared his northern campaign and [General Howe]({{ '/news/military/howe-strategy-1777/' | relative_url }}) maneuvered toward Philadelphia. The raid exposed the continuing vulnerability of American logistics to British sea power and mobile striking forces. For months afterward, the Continental Army labored to replace the destroyed provisions, and the loss contributed to supply shortages through the summer of 1777.

For the local defenders, however, the raid and especially the Battle of Ridgefield became a point of pride. The militia's ability to respond rapidly, concentrate forces, and contest a professional British army demonstrated the embedded martial capacity of American civilian communities — a pattern that would repeat throughout the Revolutionary War on the coastal frontiers. The Danbury Raid thus stands as a symbol of both British strategic reach and American militia resilience.

## Significance

The Danbury Raid and the Battle of Ridgefield illuminate the Revolutionary War's peripheral theater of coastal raids and inland penetrations. While not decisive militarily, the operation disrupted American logistics, demonstrated the reach of British sea power, and revealed the capacity of militia forces to harass and contest professional soldiers. For 1777 specifically, the raid occurred in the context of Burgoyne's northern campaign and Howe's Philadelphia strategy — supporting operations meant to exploit American vulnerabilities. The tactical outcome — a militia check, but not a decisive defeat of the British force — epitomized the Revolutionary War's grinding asymmetry: British regulars could impose their will through superior tactics and discipline, but American militia, operating in home territory and supplied by civilian logistics networks, could make the cost prohibitive and the occupation unsustainable.
