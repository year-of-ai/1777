# John Paul Jones and the Birth of American Naval Command

**Category**: Military Events
**Key figures**: John Paul Jones, Esek Hopkins (Continental Navy Commander-in-Chief), Benjamin Franklin, John Hancock (President of Congress)

## Summary

John Paul Jones emerged in 1777 as the first prominent captain of the fledgling Continental Navy, establishing the professional officer corps and operational command that would transform American naval power. Born John Paul on July 6, 1747, near Kirkbean, Kirkcudbrightshire, Scotland — he added the surname "Jones" after fleeing to Virginia in 1773 — Jones arrived in the American colonies that same year and secured his appointment to command the newly built Continental sloop-of-war Ranger on June 14, 1777 — the same day Congress passed the Flag Resolution establishing the Stars and Stripes. Ranger, launched at Portsmouth, New Hampshire on May 10, 1777, was a compact, fast warship of roughly 308 tons burthen carrying eighteen 6-pound guns and a crew of approximately 140 men. The simultaneous adoption of the national flag and the appointment of one of the Navy's most celebrated officers to command invested 1777 with dual symbolic weight: the United States was acquiring both its emblems and its combatants.

The Continental Congress, desperate to exploit British vulnerabilities at sea, authorized letters of marque for both state and Continental vessels and invested operational command in officers like Jones who possessed both nautical expertise and the temperament to conduct commerce raiding under international law. By 1777, the Continental Navy had only about thirty vessels against Britain's hundreds of warships, making conventional fleet combat suicidal; Jones's appointment represented a deliberate shift toward professional naval officers capable of conducting audacious single-ship and small-squadron operations. His appointment preceded his most famous action — the April 24, 1778 engagement between USS Ranger and HMS Drake off Carrickfergus, Ireland, the first British warship ever captured by an American vessel — but the 1777 commissioning created the operational scaffolding enabling those raids.

## The Flag Resolution and Jones's Commission — June 14, 1777

June 14, 1777, was a day of intertwined symbolic and institutional actions by the Continental Congress in Philadelphia. The Flag Resolution, passed at the same session that formalized Jones's Continental Navy commission, specified that the new flag bear thirteen stripes alternating red and white and thirteen white stars on a field of blue — one for each state. That Jones received his commission on the very day that nation received its visual symbol underscored the Congress's intent to build parallel institutional and cultural foundations simultaneously.

Jones was not yet the commanding officer Congress envisioned as supreme naval authority; that role belonged to Esek Hopkins, the first Commander-in-Chief of the Continental Navy, whom Congress had dismissed in January 1778. Jones instead occupied the role of frontline combat commander — a naval captain authorized to seek out British merchant shipping and military vessels in European and Atlantic waters and to project American naval presence far beyond the coast. His June 14 commission was the instrument that converted his status from privateer-adjacent volunteer to an officer of the sovereign state.

## USS Ranger: Armament, Crew, and Capabilities

The Ranger, which Jones was appointed to command on June 14, 1777, embodied the Continental Navy's compromise between speed and firepower. Built across the Piscataqua at Kittery, Maine and launched on May 10, 1777, Ranger measured approximately 116 feet on deck, displaced around 308 tons burthen, and mounted eighteen 6-pound cannon — modest by British Royal Navy standards but sufficient to overpower unescorted merchantmen and lightly armed vessels. Her crew of roughly 140 men included a volatile mix of New England seamen, experienced merchant mariners, and soldiers transferred to naval service, presenting Jones with an early command challenge rooted in the Navy's incomplete professionalization.

Jones sailed Ranger from Portsmouth, New Hampshire, on November 1, 1777, bound for France — arriving at Nantes on December 2, 1777. There, he received the first official acknowledgment by a foreign power of the American flag: on February 14, 1778, the French fleet under Admiral La Motte Picquet rendered a nine-gun salute to Ranger in Quiberon Bay, the first time any foreign nation formally recognized the Stars and Stripes. This diplomatic gesture, engineered partly through Benjamin Franklin's intervention, marked the USS Ranger's transformation from a military vessel into an instrument of American diplomatic legitimacy.

## Strategic Context: Commerce Raiding vs. Fleet Action

The Continental Navy's strategic doctrine in 1777 was shaped by inescapable arithmetic: Britain maintained an active fleet of over 450 warships, while the Continental Navy could muster fewer than 35 vessels in any serviceable condition. Fleet action on equal terms was impossible; commerce raiding (guerre de course) offered the only viable naval strategy. Jones understood this and embraced it with unusual ferocity, proposing not merely to intercept British merchantmen but to raid British home ports — a psychological escalation that no American naval officer before him had seriously proposed.

His 1777–1778 approach prefigured the April 1778 raid on Whitehaven, Cumberland — Jones's most provocative operation, in which he personally led an armed party ashore in Britain itself to spike harbor guns and set fire to shipping. The Whitehaven raid caused modest material damage but enormous psychological effect: for the first time since the Dutch raid on the Medway in 1667, a foreign naval force had attacked English soil. The conceptual foundations of this audacity were laid in 1777, when Jones worked to provision, train, and configure Ranger for precisely these offensive operations.

## Significance

The 1777 birth of American naval command under Jones's stewardship proved consequential for the Revolutionary War's trajectory. American naval operations, previously limited to privateers and hastily armed merchant vessels, acquired professional direction and a centralized command structure capable of coordinated long-range operations. Jones's USS Ranger and his later flagship, the USS Bonhomme Richard, became symbols of American resistance at sea — the Bonhomme Richard's September 23, 1779 battle with HMS Serapis producing Jones's legendary retort "I have not yet begun to fight."

Beyond 1777, Jones's raids against British merchant shipping demonstrated that American naval vessels could escape the Continental Army's geographic constraints and strike enemy targets globally. His operations helped justify French investment in American naval support and contributed to the diplomatic momentum that secured French naval intervention in 1778. The June 1777 appointment and the operations it launched thus anchored the institutional memory of American naval combat and established the lineage of the United States Navy itself — Jones is today honored as the "Father of the American Navy."
