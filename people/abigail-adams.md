# Abigail Adams

**Category**: Key Figures
**Key figures**: Abigail Adams (1744–1818), John Adams, Mercy Otis Warren, James Lovell

## Summary

Abigail Adams (born 11 November 1744 in Weymouth, Massachusetts) spent 1777 at the family farm in Braintree, Massachusetts, managing household and agricultural affairs while her husband John Adams served as a delegate to the Continental Congress in Philadelphia. Throughout the year she maintained an extensive correspondence — with John Adams, with her friend Mercy Otis Warren, and with Continental Congressman James Lovell — providing detailed eyewitness accounts of the war's impact on the New England home front. Her letters discussed wartime hardships including food shortages, the scarcity of flour and salt, Continental currency inflation that eroded purchasing power month by month, and the anxieties of living within potential reach of British forces operating along the coast. The year brought deep personal tragedy: on 11 July 1777, she gave birth to a stillborn daughter, whom she named Elizabeth. Through sorrow and logistical crisis alike, her writing remained analytically sharp, politically engaged, and emotionally direct.

## Life at Braintree During the Revolution

Braintree in 1777 was simultaneously a place of relative safety and of grinding wartime austerity. With John Adams absent in Philadelphia from January through November, Abigail managed the farm, supervised hired hands and household servants, negotiated prices with local traders, educated her children, and navigated the social obligations expected of the wife of a prominent Massachusetts patriot. She planted and harvested crops, monitored livestock, and sold produce from the farm — activities she described in precise agricultural detail in her letters.

Continental currency, issued in great quantities to finance the war, lost value steadily throughout 1777. Abigail's correspondence records the practical consequences: ordinary goods cost dramatically more in paper money than they had in sterling or hard coin the previous year. She wrote to John Adams of paying inflated prices for salt (a critical preservative), of flour shortages caused by the military's requisitioning of grain vessels, and of the difficulty of obtaining coffee or sugar at any price. These observations make her letters a ground-level economic history of Revolutionary America that complements the political and military record held at places such as the [Continental Congress debates over the Articles of Confederation](../politics/articles-of-confederation.md).

Her management of the farm without a male head of household was not uncommon in wartime New England — thousands of women across the colonies performed similar roles — but Abigail Adams brought to it a reflective intelligence that she communicated through letters rather than keeping private. She did not regard domestic management and intellectual engagement as separate spheres.

## Key Correspondence of 1777

Abigail Adams wrote and received dozens of letters in 1777, many of which are preserved at the Massachusetts Historical Society through the Adams Papers project. Several themes recur:

**Military observations**: Abigail tracked the movements of British forces with remarkable precision for someone operating on rumor and traveler's reports. She reported hearing cannon fire from distant engagements and monitored the disposition of Continental Army units in Massachusetts and surrounding states. Her account of civilian responses to news from the [Battles of Saratoga](../military/battles-of-saratoga.md) — the decisive American victory in October 1777 that ultimately brought France into the war — reflects both relief and cautious optimism. She understood that a French alliance, which she hoped Saratoga would produce, might relieve pressure on the home front by threatening Britain's global commitments.

**Political commentary**: Abigail engaged substantively with the congressional debates John Adams reported. She had followed the drafting and ratification of the [Articles of Confederation](../politics/articles-of-confederation.md) with interest, understanding that the structure of the new national government would shape the independence she had championed. Her observations about the weakness of the confederal structure presaged criticisms that would fuel the Constitutional Convention a decade later.

**Correspondence with James Lovell**: A flirtatious, intellectually charged exchange with Congressman James Lovell of Massachusetts — a fellow Continental Congress delegate and admirer — formed one of the year's notable epistolary relationships. Lovell, who had studied ancient languages, engaged Abigail on classical literature and the theory of republican government. Their letters illustrate the breadth of Abigail's intellectual range and the social network of correspondence through which educated women participated in the revolutionary public sphere despite formal exclusion from its institutions.

## Personal Tragedy: Death of Elizabeth Adams

On 11 July 1777, Abigail Adams gave birth to a stillborn daughter. The child, whom she named Elizabeth (sometimes recorded as Eliza), was the sixth pregnancy Abigail had carried. The loss followed a difficult wartime winter and early summer during which Abigail had managed the farm largely alone while also nursing a protracted illness. She wrote to John Adams of the grief with restrained but unmistakable anguish, noting the cruel timing alongside the larger uncertainties of the war. The letter reached John Adams in Philadelphia, where he responded with grief and expressions of his inability to be present. The death of Elizabeth is one of the most documented instances of infant mortality in the Adams correspondence and has been examined by historians of early American family life.

## Political Advocacy and Intellectual Life

Abigail Adams had advocated for the legal and social position of women in correspondence predating 1777 — her famous "Remember the Ladies" letter to John Adams dated 31 March 1776 had urged the Continental Congress to incorporate protections for women in whatever new legal framework they constructed. By 1777 she understood that no such provisions had been made, and her later letters grew increasingly sharp about the contradiction between revolutionary rhetoric of equality and the legal subordination of women.

She was also a consistent advocate for public education, particularly for girls. Self-educated — she never attended formal school, an absence she sometimes lamented — she had read widely in English history, French literature, and classical thought. She corresponded with her sister Mary Smith Cranch about books and ideas, and she followed political philosophy closely enough to form independent views about the comparative merits of different systems of government. Her parallel to [George Washington](george-washington.md)'s leadership at [Valley Forge](../military/valley-forge.md) is instructive: both exemplified the stoic endurance demanded of all ranks of Revolutionary society, in very different registers.

## Legacy and Historical Significance

Abigail Adams's 1777 letters survive as among the richest eyewitness accounts of the American home front during the Revolutionary War. Together with the letters that preceded and followed them, they form part of an extraordinary exchange with John Adams comprising roughly 1,160 letters across more than three decades — one of the most extensive and historically significant bodies of personal correspondence in American history, preserved at the Massachusetts Historical Society.

She became the second First Lady of the United States (1797–1801) and the mother of the sixth president, John Quincy Adams. Historians of early America have increasingly recognized her as an intellectual force whose influence on her husband's thinking was substantive rather than merely supportive, and whose own political observations and advocacy placed her among the most significant women of the founding era. The stillborn Elizabeth of 1777 is a reminder that behind the public record of war and constitution-making lay private lives of enormous fragility and cost.
