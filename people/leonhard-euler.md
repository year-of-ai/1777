# Leonhard Euler — Mathematics and Science Despite Blindness

**Category**: Key Figures

**Key figures**: Leonhard Euler, Catherine the Great (patron), Daniel Bernoulli (mentor and friend)

## Summary

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783), one of history's most prolific mathematicians and scientists, continued his extraordinary intellectual output in 1777 despite being almost entirely blind — producing, on average, one mathematical paper per week while residing in Saint Petersburg under the patronage of the Russian Academy of Sciences. His work during this period spanned number theory, analysis, fluid dynamics, celestial mechanics, and other fields, establishing a standard of scientific productivity that has never been surpassed and demonstrating that intellectual achievement could transcend physical limitation.

## Life and Career

Born on April 15, 1707, in Basel, Switzerland, Euler trained under Johann Bernoulli, the leading mathematician of the day, and received his master's degree from the University of Basel at age 16. He joined the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1727 and published his first major treatise, *Mechanica* (1736), a systematic reformulation of Newtonian mechanics in terms of differential equations — a transformation that made Newton's methods vastly more applicable.

After spending 25 years in Berlin (1741–1766) under the patronage of Frederick the Great at the Berlin Academy of Sciences, Euler returned to Saint Petersburg in 1766 at the personal invitation of Catherine the Great, who provided him a house, a pension, and a cook. She reportedly considered his presence an ornament to her reign.

## Progressive Blindness and Intellectual Triumph

Euler lost sight in his right eye in 1738, likely from a fever, and sight in his left eye following a cataract surgery in 1766 — the surgery intended to restore vision instead left him nearly totally blind within weeks. He was 59 years old.

Rather than curtailing his output, blindness paradoxically clarified Euler's mathematical method. With no ability to write himself, he dictated to scribes — including his son Johann Albrecht Euler — compositions of extraordinary density and precision. Euler possessed a prodigious memory: he could recite the entire *Aeneid* by heart, and reportedly once calculated the first six powers of all integers up to 100 while his scribes awaited dictation. His mental arithmetic was such that he could solve complex computations faster than scribes could write down the figures.

From 1775 onward, Euler "produced, on average, one mathematical paper per week" — a rate sustained through 1777 and continuing until his death in September 1783. At the time of his death, 228 of his papers were awaiting publication; the Saint Petersburg Academy published his backlog for the next 47 years.

## Mathematical Notation and Language

One of Euler's most enduring contributions, largely consolidated in the period before 1777 but still actively in use during it, was the mathematical language now universally employed:

- **e** for the base of natural logarithms (approximately 2.71828…), first used in *Mechanica* (1736)
- **i** for the imaginary unit √(-1), introduced in 1777 in his memoir "De formulis differentialibus angularibus" (on angular differential formulas), presented to the Saint Petersburg Academy
- **π** for the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, popularized though not coined by Euler
- **Σ** (sigma) for summation notation, and **f(x)** for function notation
- **sin** and **cos** as abbreviations for trigonometric functions

The introduction of *i* for the imaginary unit in 1777 is particularly notable: Euler's formalization of complex number notation in that year gave mathematics a tool that would become essential to the development of electrical engineering, quantum mechanics, and signal processing centuries later. His identity *e^(iπ) + 1 = 0* — encapsulating five fundamental mathematical constants in one equation — is considered by many mathematicians the most beautiful equation in mathematics.

## Scientific and Mathematical Contributions

During 1777 and the surrounding years, Euler's work encompassed:

- **Number Theory**: investigation of prime numbers, Fermat's Last Theorem (Euler proved it for the case n=3), quadratic reciprocity (partially proved), and the distribution of primes
- **Analysis**: advancement of the calculus of variations, infinite series, and what became known as Eulerian integrals (the Gamma function and Beta function)
- **Physics and Fluid Dynamics**: the Euler equations governing inviscid fluid flow, which remain fundamental to aerodynamics and hydrodynamics
- **Celestial Mechanics**: lunar theory and the three-body problem, contributing to navigation tables used by maritime powers including Britain during the Revolutionary War
- **Optics**: the *Lettres à une Princesse d'Allemagne* (Letters to a German Princess), a series of popular science letters on optics, mechanics, and natural philosophy completed earlier but still widely read

## The Saint Petersburg Context in 1777

The city that housed Euler in 1777 was one of the great intellectual centers of the Enlightenment. Catherine the Great's Russia was a patron of the arts and sciences on a European scale, and the Russian Academy of Sciences housed some of the continent's leading minds. Euler's presence there — corresponding by letter with Lagrange, d'Alembert, Daniel Bernoulli, and other leading mathematicians across Europe — made Saint Petersburg a node in the international network of Enlightenment science even as Atlantic powers fought over American independence.

Euler's mathematical correspondence with Lagrange in Paris and with other Continental mathematicians in 1777 helped maintain the flow of mathematical knowledge across national boundaries during a period of military conflict, embodying the cosmopolitan ideal of the Republic of Letters.

## Significance

Leonhard Euler's continued mathematical and scientific work in 1777 stands as one of the most remarkable demonstrations of intellectual perseverance in history. His total published output — 866 papers and books according to the Eneström index — exceeds that of any other mathematician in history. The Saint Petersburg Academy spent nearly half a century publishing his accumulated manuscripts after his death.

In a year marked by military conflict, political transformation, and social upheaval across the Atlantic and European worlds, Euler represented the continued advancement of pure and applied science through disciplined intellectual work. The imaginary unit *i*, which Euler introduced formally in 1777, would prove essential to the mathematics of the industrial and electronic revolutions that followed — a quiet contribution whose long-term consequences for technology rivaled those of any battle fought that year.

His productivity in 1777 underscores the reality that scientific progress occurred simultaneously with and independently of the Revolutionary War, driven by researchers like [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](../science/coulomb-electrostatics.md), [Antoine Lavoisier](../science/lavoisier-oxygen-theory.md), [William Herschel](../science/herschel-telescopes-deep-sky.md), and Euler himself — whose work would shape the emerging modern world as profoundly as any military victory.
